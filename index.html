WHEN TO MEDIATE

Earlier is usually better.

A dispute can be mediated at any stage: before anyone files, during litigation or arbitration, or after judgment while an appeal is pending. The earliest point is often the best one, while the relationships and the business still have value.

Mediation is also useful outside formal disputes. Where a negotiation has stalled, or a separation is better negotiated than litigated, a neutral can keep the discussion moving.

The process

From positions to a reasoned assessment of risk.

1

Preparation

Before the session I speak with counsel for each side, separately and in confidence, to understand what is in dispute and what is driving it. Each party submits a brief, either exchanged or for my eyes only, about ten days before the session. I read everything and, where it helps, do my own work on the legal and commercial questions.

2

Joint session

Where it serves the parties, the day opens together. Each side sets out how it sees the matter, and I summarise each account back so that everyone knows it has been heard accurately. The aim at this stage is clarity about what is keeping the parties apart.

3

Private sessions

I then meet each side separately and in confidence. This is where risks are tested candidly, where information can be shared without weakening a negotiating position, and where options beyond money come into view.

4

Closing

When the movement slows, I may offer a mediator's proposal, to which each side responds in confidence. A term sheet is signed before anyone leaves, so the agreement reached on the day is the agreement that is documented.

5

Follow through

If a matter does not resolve on the day, I stay with it. Many settlements close in the week after a session.

Protocol

What to expect.

Starting a mediation

Either party, or counsel, can make the first approach. If you are unsure how to raise mediation with the other side, contact me and I will help you propose it.

Decision makers

The single greatest factor in reaching resolution is the attendance of the people with real authority to settle.

Briefs

Please keep briefs to ten pages, with only the exhibits that decide the matter. Attach the governing agreements and the documents on which the matter turns.

Format

Sessions take place anywhere in the world, in person or by video, in English or French. Most matters are scheduled as a single day.

Confidentiality

Every mediation is confidential. The parties sign a confidentiality agreement before the session, and in California, communications are also protected by Evidence Code sections 1115 to 1128.

Neutrality

Before accepting any matter I run a conflicts check and disclose anything a reasonable party would want to know. As a mediator I do not represent or give legal advice to any party. I follow the California Rules of Court standards of conduct for mediators in court connected matters and apply the same standards to private ones.

Questions

Frequently asked.

Is mediation binding?

The process is voluntary and either party can leave at any time. A settlement agreement signed at the end of mediation is a binding contract, and it can be drafted to be enforceable in court.

Do we need lawyers?

Not always, though in most matters each side benefits from counsel. Parties who attend without counsel can have the agreement reviewed before signing.

Can you mediate a dispute already in litigation or arbitration?

Yes. Mediation can run alongside a pending case, and many courts and arbitration rules encourage it.

What if one side is far more powerful than the other?

Imbalances of resources and information are common. Private sessions let each side be heard on its own terms, and my role is to make sure each decision is an informed one.

Can you serve as fractional general counsel for a company outside California or New York?

Often, yes. Much of a general counsel's work is transactional and regulatory rather than tied to one state's courts, and where local admission is required I work with local counsel. We settle this in the engagement letter.

Can you act as both mediator and counsel?

No. A mediator is a neutral. I will not mediate a dispute involving a current or former client, and I will not represent a party to a dispute I have mediated.
